Mr. Bennet was among the earliest of those who waited on Mr. Bingley. He had always intended to visit him, though to the last always assuring his wife that he should not go; and till the evening after the visit was paid she had no knowledge of it. It was then disclosed in the following manner. Observing his second daughter employed in trimming a hat, he suddenly addressed her with:

"I hope Mr. Bingley will like it, Lizzy."

"We are not in a way to know what Mr. Bingley likes," said her mother resentfully, "since we are not to visit."

"But you forget, mamma," said Elizabeth, "that we shall meet him at the assemblies, and Mrs. Long promised to introduce him."

"I do not believe Mrs. Long will do any such thing. She has two nieces of her own. She is a selfish, hypocritical woman, and I have no opinion of her."

"No more have I," said Mr. Bennet; "and I am glad to find that you do not depend on her serving you."

Mrs. Bennet deigned not to make any reply, but, unable to contain herself, she began scolding one of her daughters.

"Don't keep coughing so, Kitty, for heaven's sake! Have a little compassion on my nerves. You tear them to pieces."

"Mamma, I do not cough."

"You haven't made up your mind about going yet?" said Elizabeth.

Mr. Bennet went on looking at his book.

"No," said his wife, "I will not go. You may take the girls yourself."

But this scheme did not please Elizabeth. She had a great opinion of her own beauty, and was very anxious that Mr. Bingley should see her. She would not have been sorry if he had been at all like Mr. Bingley's sister's description of him; but it was not till the third day after his arrival that she saw him, and then only for a few minutes, at the Meryton assembly.

The ladies of whom she had heard, without ever seeing their husband or his friend for many months, made her resolution to hear his name again very soon. At length the day came when she was to be introduced to him at the assembly. Her mother had the first dance with Mr. Collins; Mr. Bingley danced with Jane, and Mr. Darcy with Elizabeth. But Elizabeth was saved from all solicitude about her partner by the following occurrence. Mr. Bingley asked her to dance, and Elizabeth was delighted; but when her mother came up and said, "Lizzy, here is Mr. Darcy," she was forced to decline. Her sister's partner was the only one she cared about.

Mr. Bingley was good-looking and gentlemanlike; he had a pleasant countenance, and easy, unaffected manners. His sisters were fine women, with an air of decided fashion. His brother-in-law, Mr. Hurst, merely looked the gentleman; but his friend Mr. Darcy soon drew the attention of the room by his fine, tall person, handsome features, noble mien, and the report which was in general circulation within five minutes after his entrance, of his having ten thousand a year. The gentlemen pronounced him to be a fine figure of a man, the ladies declared he was much handsomer than Mr. Bingley, and he was looked at with great admiration for about half the evening, till his manners gave a disgust which turned the tide of his popularity; for he was discovered to be proud; to be above his company, and above being pleased; and not all his large estate in Derbyshire could then save him from having a most forbidding, disagreeable countenance, and being unworthy to be compared with his friend.

Mr. Bingley had soon made himself acquainted with all the principal people in the room; he was lively and unreserved, danced every dance, was angry that the ball closed so early, and talked of giving one himself at Netherfield. Such amiable qualities must speak for themselves. What a contrast between him and his friend! Mr. Darcy danced only once with Mrs. Hurst and once with Miss Bingley, declined being introduced to any other lady, and spent the rest of the evening in walking about the room, speaking occasionally to one of his own party. His character was decided. He was the proudest, most disagreeable man in the world, and everybody hoped that he would never come there again. Amongst the most violent against him was Mrs. Bennet, whose dislike of his general behaviour was sharpened into particular resentment by his having slighted one of her daughters.

Elizabeth related to Jane the next day what had passed between Mr. Wickham and herself. But Jane, who knew nothing of the matter, could not tell what to think. "Oh!" said Elizabeth, "I am not at all surprised. You were always talking of his extravagance. And did you know what he did about the living?"

"I heard something about it," said Jane, "but I did not know what it was."

"He told me," said Elizabeth, "that he had been presented to it by Mr. Darcy."

"And who is Mr. Darcy?"

"A very agreeable man, I assure you. And what do you think? He says Mr. Wickham is the worst man he ever knew."

"The worst man he ever knew! And pray what is he?"

"He is the most agreeable man I ever saw."

"You are both very strange. But what is Mr. Wickham's story?"

"You must ask my aunt," said Elizabeth, "for I have not the least idea."

"You are very polite. I am sure I wish I knew."

"I am sure I do not. But, my dear Jane, you must not let your mother know that Mr. Wickham is gone. She will be quite distracted."

"I am sure she will not be sorry to hear it."
