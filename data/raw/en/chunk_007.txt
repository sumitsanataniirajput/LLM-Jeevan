Jane looked well, and Elizabeth had little opportunity of studying her spirits, amidst the various engagements which the kindness of her aunt had reserved for them. But Jane was to go home with her, and at Longbourn there would be leisure enough for observation.

It was not without an effort, meanwhile, that she could wait even for Longbourn, before she told her sister of Mr. Darcy's proposals. To know that she had the power of revealing what would so exceedingly astonish Jane, and must, at the same time, so highly gratify whatever of her own vanity she had not yet been able to reason away, was such a temptation to openness as nothing could have conquered, but the state of indecision in which she remained as to the extent of what she should communicate, and her fear, if she once entered on the subject, of being hurried into repeating something of Bingley, which might only grieve her sister further.

She told her sister, however, all that had passed between Mr. Darcy and herself, except the contents of his letter, which she had determined to keep secret. Jane listened with the greatest astonishment. She could not believe that Mr. Darcy had made an offer to her sister. She could not believe that her sister had refused him.

"But you refused him, Lizzy?" said Jane.

"Yes, I refused him," said Elizabeth.

"And why?"

"Because I did not love him," said Elizabeth.

"And you never will love him?"

"I never shall love him," said Elizabeth.

"But he is a very agreeable man," said Jane.

"He is a very disagreeable man," said Elizabeth.

"I am sorry to hear you say so," said Jane. "I am sure he is a very good man."

"He may be a good man, but he is not agreeable," said Elizabeth.

"I am very sorry," said Jane. "I am sure you will be very sorry too, some day."

"I hope not," said Elizabeth.

When Elizabeth had told Jane all that had passed, she walked with her to the garden, and they sat down together under a tree. Elizabeth told Jane everything, except the letter. She told Jane how Mr. Darcy had proposed to her, and how she had refused him. She told Jane how she had felt when she read his letter, and how she had changed her mind about him. Jane listened very carefully. She was very surprised to hear that Mr. Darcy had proposed to Elizabeth. She was also very surprised to hear that Elizabeth had refused him. But she was glad to hear that Elizabeth had changed her mind about him. She thought that Mr. Darcy was a very good man, and that Elizabeth would be very happy with him.

"I am very glad, Lizzy," said Jane, "that you have changed your mind about Mr. Darcy. I am sure he is a very good man, and that you will be very happy with him."

"I hope so, Jane," said Elizabeth. "But I am not sure yet. I need to think about it more."

"You must not think too much," said Jane. "You must follow your heart."

"I will try," said Elizabeth.
