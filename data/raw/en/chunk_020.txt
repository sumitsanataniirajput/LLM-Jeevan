Elizabeth read this letter with the most comfortable feelings of gratification. Her sister’s happiness was now all she desired, and she had the fullest assurance of Mr. Darcy’s regard; yet she could not but feel that it would be impossible for her ever to be happy with him, after what had passed between them. She could not help thinking that he must have been deeply hurt by her rejection of him, and that he would never be able to forget it.

She was roused from these reflections by the entrance of her uncle and aunt, who came to tell her that Lydia and Wickham were coming to Longbourn that day, and to invite her to be present at the marriage settlement. Elizabeth gladly accepted the invitation, and they all set off together in the carriage.

On their arrival at Longbourn, they found that Mr. Bennet had just returned from London, and was in the library writing letters. He received them with his usual good-humour, and inquired after their journey. When he heard that Lydia and Wickham were coming, he seemed much pleased, and said that he would like to see them both.

The marriage settlement was soon drawn up, and Mr. Wickham’s commission was purchased by Mr. Gardiner. Everything was settled in a very short time, and Lydia and Wickham were married the next day at St. Clement’s Church.

After the wedding, the happy couple returned to Longbourn, where they remained for a fortnight. Mrs. Bennet was in raptures, and went about telling everyone that her daughter was married. Mr. Bennet, however, was not so pleased, and he made it clear that he would not receive them at Longbourn again.

Lydia and Wickham left Longbourn at the end of the fortnight, and went to join Wickham’s regiment in the north. Before they left, Lydia came to say goodbye to Elizabeth, and to thank her for all her kindness. Elizabeth wished her well, but she could not help feeling that Lydia had made a very foolish match.

When Lydia and Wickham were gone, Elizabeth’s thoughts naturally turned to Mr. Darcy. She had not seen him since his visit to Gracechurch Street, and she wondered what he was doing and where he was. She had received no letter from him, and she began to think that he had forgotten her.

One day, about a fortnight after Lydia’s marriage, Elizabeth was walking in the garden with Jane, when they saw Mr. Bingley and Mr. Darcy coming towards them. Mr. Bingley was very glad to see them, and he stopped to talk with them for a few minutes. Mr. Darcy, however, seemed to be in a hurry, and he passed them without speaking.

Elizabeth was disappointed, but she tried not to show it. She walked on with Jane, and they talked about Mr. Bingley and Mr. Darcy, and about the prospects of their marriage. Jane was hopeful, but Elizabeth was more doubtful.

“Do you think that Mr. Bingley will ever propose to you?” she asked.

“I hope so,” said Jane, “but I am not sure. He has been very kind to me, but he has not said anything about marriage.”

“I think that he likes you very much,” said Elizabeth, “but I am afraid that his sisters will interfere, as they did before.”

“I hope not,” said Jane, “but I cannot be certain.”

They continued their walk, and soon they reached the end of the garden. They turned back, and on their way they met Mr. Darcy again. This time he stopped and spoke to Elizabeth.

“I hope that you are well,” he said.

“Thank you,” said Elizabeth, “I am quite well.”

“I am glad to hear it,” said Mr. Darcy. “I have been thinking about what happened at Gracechurch Street, and I hope that you will forgive me for what I said.”

“I have already forgiven you,” said Elizabeth, “and I hope that you will forgive me for what I said.”

“I do forgive you,” said Mr. Darcy, “and I hope that we may be friends again.”

“I should like that very much,” said Elizabeth.

They walked on together for a few minutes, talking about various things, and then Mr. Darcy said that he must go. He bowed to Elizabeth and Jane, and then he walked away.

Elizabeth watched him go, and she felt a strange mixture of emotions. She was glad that he had spoken to her, but she was sorry that he was leaving. She wondered if she would ever see him again.

A few days later, Elizabeth received a letter from Mr. Darcy. It was a short letter, and it said nothing about his feelings for her, but it asked her to come to Pemberley to visit him. Elizabeth was surprised, but she accepted the invitation, and she set off for Pemberley the next day.

When she arrived at Pemberley, she was received by Mr. Darcy himself, who seemed very glad to see her. He showed her round the house and the grounds, and they talked about many things. Elizabeth was struck by the change in Mr. Darcy. He was no longer proud and reserved, but kind and courteous. She began to think that she might have been mistaken about him.

After a few days at Pemberley, Elizabeth returned to Longbourn. She had not told Mr. Darcy that she loved him, but she knew that she did. She also knew that he loved her, and she hoped that they would soon be married.
